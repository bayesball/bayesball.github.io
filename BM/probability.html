2.1 Introduction

The goal of this chapter is to introduce probability by covering several common probability distributions. A random variable is a number assigned to the outcome of a experiment where the outcome is not known in advance. In the case where the random variable can only assume a countable number of values, the associated probabilities can described by a discrete probability distribution. Section 2.2 introduces the construction of probabilities for a discrete random variable and introduces the Binomial and Negative Binomial distributions which are based on a coin-tossing experiment.

In the situation where the random variable takes on a continuum of values, probabilities are represented as areas under a density curve. Section 2.3 introduces continuous random variables by use of spinner and waiting time examples. This section introduces the Normal density which is a common function for representing a measurement random variable.

The following sections describe joint probability distributions for two random variables. Section 2.4 describes probability distributions for two discrete random variables and Section 2.5 describes the use of a density curve to represent probabilities for two continuous random variables. In this setting, Section 2.6 discusses how these joint probability distributions can be used to extract marginal probability distributions for a single variable. Section 2.7 describes the construction of probability distributions for one variable conditional on a value of the second variable.

2.2 Discrete Probability Distributions

2.2.1 A Simple Coin Game

Suppose that Peter and Paul play a simple coin game. A coin is tossed. If the coin lands heads, then Peter receives $2 from Paul; otherwise Peter has to pay $2 to Paul. The game is played for a total of five coin flips. After the five flips, what is Peter’s net gain (in dollars)?

This is an illustration of a random experiment where the outcome of the flips is unknown. For each possible outcome of the flips, say \(HTHHT\), there will be a corresponding net gain for Peter. For this outcome, Peter won three times and lost twice, so his net gain is \(3(2) - 2(2) = 2\) dollars. The net gain is an example of a random variable – a number assigned to each outcome of the random experiment. Generally, a capital letter will be used to represent a random variable – here the capital letter \(G\) denotes Peter’s gain in this experiment.

The next task is to assign probabilities for the values of the random variable. There are \(2 ^ 5 = 32\) possible outcomes of this experiment and since these outcomes are equally likely, we assign to each outcome a probability of 1/32. We can compute the probability of a value for \(G\) by counting the number of outcomes corresponding to that value and divide the number by 32. For example, the five outcomes where Peter’s net gain is \(G = 6\) are

HHHHT, HHHTH, HHTHH, HTHHH, THHHH

and so \(P(G = 6)\) is 5/32.

If we enumerate these 32 outcomes and calculate the associated net gain value for each outcome, we see there are 6 possible values of \(G\): -10, -6, -2, 2, 6, and 10 dollars. By counting the number of outcomes for each value of \(G\) and dividing the count by 32, we can compute the probability of all values. If we list the values of \(G\) together with the associated probabilities, we obtain a probability distribution for \(G\) as displayed in Table 2.1.

2.2.2 Discrete Random Variable

In general, \(X\) is a discrete random variable if it only assigns probability to a discrete set of values. The function \(f(x)\) is a probability mass function (pmf) for \(X\) if the function satisfies two properties.

  • \(f(x) \ge 0\) for each possible value \(x\) of \(X\)
  • \(\sum_x f(x) = 1\) (the probabilities sum to 1 over all values of \(X\))

A discrete probability distribution is a listing of the values of \(X\) together with the associated values of the pmf. We graphically displays this probability distribution with a bar graph. All of the values of \(G\) are placed on the horizontal axis, a probability scale is marked on the vertical scale, and then vertical lines are drawn on the graph corresponding to the pmf values. Figure 2.1 displays the graph of the probability distribution of \(G\) in this example.

2.2.3 Mean and Standard Deviation of a Discrete Random Variable

A probability distribution of a discrete random variable can be used to find probabilities of interest. For example, what is the probability Peter wins money in this game? Peter wins money if the gain \(G\) is positive and this corresponds to the values \(G = 2, 6, 10\). By adding up the probabilities of these three values, we see the probability that Peter wins money is

\[\begin{align*} P({ Peter \, \, wins}) &= P(G > 0) \\ &= P(G = 2) + P(G = 6) + P(G = 10) \\ &= \frac{10 + 5+1}{32} = \frac{1}{2}. \end{align*}\]

A common measure of “average” of a probability distribution is the mean or expected value of \(X\), denoted \(\mu\) or \(E(X)\). The mean (or expected value) is found by weighting each value of \(X\) by its associated probability and summing over all values of \(X\):

\[\begin{equation} \mu = \sum_x x f(x). \end{equation}\]

Two measures of spread of a random variable \(X\) are the variance, denoted by \(\sigma^2\), and the standard deviation denoted by \(\sigma\). To compute the variance, we weight the values of \((x - \mu)^2\) by the associated probabilities of \(X\) and find the sum of the products. \[\begin{equation} \sigma^2 = \Sigma_x (x - \mu)^2 f(x). \end{equation}\] The variance represents an “average” squared distance (or deviation) from the mean \(\mu\).

The standard deviation is the square root of the variance: \[\begin{align} \sigma & = \sqrt{\sigma^2} \nonumber \\ & = \sqrt{E(X - \mu)^2} \nonumber \\ & = \sqrt{\Sigma_x(x-\mu)^2f(x)} \end{align}\] The standard deviation is often more useful than the variance as \(\sigma\) is measured on the same scale as the random variable \(X\).

For the Peter-Paul game, the mean can be computed to be \[ \mu = -10 (1/32) -6 (5/32) -2 (10/32) + 2 (10/32) + 6 (5/32) + 10 (1/32) = 0. \]

Note that \(G = 0\) is not a possible outcome of the game – generally the mean of a random variable does not have to be a possible outcome value. But the mean or expected value has a long-run frequency interpretation. If Peter and Paul play this game a large number of times, then the value \(\mu\)= 0 represents the mean winnings of Peter in all of these games.

By use of Equation (2.2), the variance is given by

\[\begin{align*} \sigma^2 & = (-10 -0)^2 (1/32) + (-6 - 0)^2 (5/32) + (-2 - 0)^2 (10/32) + \\ & (2 - 0)^2 (10/32) + (6 - 0)^2 (5/32) + (10 - 0)^2 (1/32) = 20. \end{align*}\] and so the standard deviation is equal to \[ \sigma = \sqrt{20} = 4.47. \]

The value 4.417 represents an average or typical deviation of Peter’s gain (in dollars) from the mean value of 0. Since the value of the standard deviation is relatively large, this indicates that Peter’s actual gain in this game is uncertain, that is, there is much variability in Peter’s gain in repeated plays of the game.

2.2.4 Binomial Distribution

The Binomial distribution is one of the most commonly used distributions in Bayesian inference. It is often used as the data model for random experiments with two outcomes.

Consider the following random process called a Binomial experiment.

  1. The same basic task or trial is repeated many times – let the number of trials be denoted by \(n\).
  2. On each trial, there are two possible outcomes, which are called “success” or “failure”. Examples of such outcomes include “yes” or “no”, or “0” or “1”.
  3. The probability of a success, denoted by \(p\), is the same for each trial.
  4. The results of outcomes from different trials are independent. This means that the chance of a particular outcome does not depend on outcomes on previous trials.

We are generally interested in \(X\), the number of observed successes out of \(n\) trials in this experiment.. The probability of \(X = x\) successes is given by the Binomial formula: \[\begin{equation} P(X = x) = {{n} \choose {x}} p^x (1 - p)^{n-x}, \, \, k = x ..., n, \end{equation}\] where \({n \choose x}\) is the choose function \[\begin{equation} {n \choose x} = \frac{n!}{x! (n - x)!}. \end{equation}\]

Let’s illustrate using this formula for a familiar task of throwing a six-sided die. Suppose you roll a fair die five times and record the number of 4’s that are observed. This can be considered a Binomial experiment where the number of trials \(n = 5\) and the probability of success on a single trial is \(p = 1/6\). The random variable \(X\) is the number of rolls of 4 during this game.

Using Equation (2.4), the probability that we observe exactly two 4’s, that is \(P(X = 2)\) is equal to \[ P(X = 2) = {5 \choose 2} (1/6)^2 (1 - 1/6)^{5-2}. \] This computation in R can be directly performed by use of the choose() function.

Code 
choose(5, 2) * (1/6) ^ 2 * (1 - 1/6) ^ (5 - 2)
[1] 0.160751

Binomial probabilities can also be computed by use of the R function dbinom() that calculates the pmf of a Binomial distribution for values of the parameters \(n\) and \(p\). The inputs to dbinom() are the number of successes of interest \(x\) and the size \(n\) and prob \(p\).

Code 
dbinom(2, size = 5, prob = 1 / 6)
[1] 0.160751

What is the probability that we roll at least one 4 in this experiment? This computation can be done by using the dbinom() function to find the probabilities of the values \(X\) = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and then using the sum() function to add up these individual probabilities.

Code 
sum(dbinom(1:5, size = 5, prob = 1/6))
[1] 0.5981224

The chance of rolling at least one 4 in five rolls is 0.598.

The mean and variance of a Binomial random variable \(X\) are given by \[\begin{equation} E(X) = n p, \, \, \, Var(X) = n p (1-p). \end{equation}\]

In our dice rolling example where \(X\) denoted the number of 4’s in five rolls, the number of trials and probability of success are given by \(n\) = 5 and \(p\) = 1/6. The expected number of 4’s would be \[ E(X) = 5 (1/6) = 0.833 \] and the variance of the number of 4’s would be \[ Var(X) =5 (1/6) (1 - 1/6) = 25/36 = 0.694. \]

When the Binomial experiment is repeated many times, the mean and variance of \(X\) can be approximated by the sample mean and sample variance of the simulated draws of the random variable. In this example, we can simulate 5000 draws of five dice by use of the rbinom() function. The vector four_rolls contains the number of 4’s observed in these 5000 experiments. The mean() and var() functions are used to compute the sample mean and sample variance of these draws. Note that the values are close approximations to exact the values of \(E(X\)) and \(Var(X)\) from Equation (2.6).

Code 
four_rolls <- rbinom(5000, size = 5, prob = 1/6)
c(M = mean(four_rolls), V = var(four_rolls))
        M         V 
0.8432000 0.7091556

This is an illustration of an approximation by Monte Carlo simulation that will be seen in later chapters to be an important Bayesian computation tool.

Although the Binomial distribution is popular for representing experiments with two outcomes for its ease in probability computations, its use depends on the assumptions of a Binomial experiment. In cases where the probability of success is not constant across trials or where the trials are not independent, then alternative probability distributions should be used.

2.2.5 Negative Binomial Distribution

In a Binomial experiment, it is assumed that the number of trials is fixed. In alternative scenarios, we may be observing binary outcomes where the number of outcomes is actually random. To illustrate this situation, consider a baseball example featuring a famous Japanese hitter.

In the 2004 baseball season Ichiro Suzuki of the Seattle Mariners had the opportunity to break the record for the most hits in a single season. George Sisler’s record was 257 hits and Suzuki had 255 hits before the Mariners’ game on September 30. Was it likely that Suzuki would tie Sisler’s record during this particular game?

This process can be viewed as a coin-tossing experiment. When Suzuki comes to bat, there are two relevant outcomes: either he will get a hit, or he will get an out. Assume the probability that he gets a hit on a single at-bat is \(p\) = 0.372 (his current batting average) and the outcomes on different at-bats are independent. Suzuki needs two more hits to tie the record. How many outs will it take him to get two hits?

This is not a Binomial experiment since the number of trials is not fixed. Instead the number of successes is fixed in advance and the number of failures to achieve this is random. In this example, we are interested in probabilities about \(X\) = number of outs (failures) to get two hits (successes).

A general Negative Binomial experiment is described as follows:

  1. There is a sequence of independent trials where each trial can be a success or a failure.
  2. The probability of a success on a single trial is \(p\).
  3. The experiment is continued until we observe \(r\) successes, and \(X\) = number of observed failures.

The probability that it takes \(x\) failures to observe \(r\) successes, the pmf of \(X\), is given by \[\begin{equation} P(X = x) = {r + x -1 \choose r-1} p^r (1- p)^x, x = 0, 1, 2 ... \end{equation}\]

Let’s use R and this formula in our baseball example where \(r = 2\) and \(p\) = 0.372 and we wish to compute the probability \(X = 2\):

Code 
r <- 2
p <- .372
choose(r + 2 -1, r - 1) * p ^ r * (1 - p) ^ 2
[1] 0.1637293

The R function dnbinom() provides Negative Binomial probabilities where the first argument is x, size is the value of \(r\) and prob is the value of \(p\). Using this function, the following table gives the probabilities for the number of outs values \(x\) = 0, 1, 2, …, 7.

Code 
x <- 0:7
data.frame(x, prob = dnbinom(x, size = 2, prob = 0.372))
  x       prob
1 0 0.13838400
2 1 0.17381030
3 2 0.16372931
4 3 0.13709601
5 4 0.10762036
6 5 0.08110271
7 6 0.05942125
8 7 0.04264748

Note that it is most likely that Suzuki will get one out to get his two additional hits, but the probability of one out is only 17%. There is a significant probability that Suzuki will take more outs. We use R to add the probabilities that \(X\) is at most 7:

Code 
sum(dnbinom(0:7, size = 2, prob = 0.372))
[1] 0.9038114

We see that the probability that \(X\) is at most 7 is 0.904, so the probability that \(X\) exceeds 7 is \(1 - 0.904 = 0.096\).

For a Negative Binomial experiment where \(X\) is the number of failures needed to observe \(r\) successes, the mean and variance values are given by \[\begin{equation} E(X) = \frac{r (1 - p)}{p}, Var(X) = \frac{r (1 - p)}{p^2}. \end{equation}\] For the baseball example, \(r = 2\) and \(p = 0.372\), so the expected number of outs to get two hits would be \(E(X) = 2 (1 - 0.372) /0.372 = 3.4\). Note that although \(X = 1\) is the most probable value, Suzuki would average over 3 outs to get 2 hits in many repetitions of this random experiment.

Note that sometimes in a Negative Binomial experiment, the random variable is defined to be \(Y\), the number of trials to observe \(r\) successes. The formula for the probability distribution of \(Y\) differs from the one for \(X\), but we can perform probability calculations with either definition.

2.2.6 Poisson Distribution

Another discrete probability distribution useful in Bayesian settings is the Poisson distribution Let the random variable \(X\) denote the number of occurrences of an event in an interval with sample space \(\{0, 1, 2, \cdots \}\). The variable \(X\) follows a Poisson distribution with rate parameter \(\lambda\) when the pmf of observing \(x\) events in an interval is given by

\[\begin{eqnarray} f(X = x \mid \lambda) = e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^x}{x!}, \, \, x = 0, 1, 2, ... \end{eqnarray}\] where \(\lambda\) is the average number of events per interval, \(e = 2.71828...\) is Euler’s number, and \(x!\) is the factorial of \(x\).

The Poisson sampling model is based on the following assumptions about the sampling process:

  1. It is assumed that the time interval is fixed.

  2. Counts of arrivals occurring during different time intervals are independent.

  3. The rate \(\lambda\) at which the arrivals occur is constant over time.

To check the suitability of the Poisson distribution, we need to check these conditions for the particular application.

Consider am example where a hospital wants to determine how many doctors and nurses to assign on their emergency room (ER) team between 10pm and 11pm during the week. An important piece of information is the count of patients arriving in the ER in this one-hour period. For this example, the event is a patient’s arrival to the ER, and the fixed time interval is the period between 10pm and 11pm.

In this ER example it is reasonable to assume that the time of one patient’s arrival does not influence the time of another patient’s arrival. So it is assumed that events occur independently during different time intervals. In addition, we might not think that the rate of patient arrivals would change much through one hour during the evening, so it seems reasonable to assume that the average number of events is constant in the fixed interval.

Suppose that 20 patients on average arrive in the ER in the one-hour period, so we set \(\lambda = 20\). The random variable \(X\) is the number of patients arriving in the interval. What is the probability that exactly 15 patients arrive? We apply the Poisson pmf in Equation (2.9):

\[ P(X = 15) = e^{-20} \frac{20^{15}}{15!} \] The R function dpois() computes Poisson probabilities where the first argument is x and the second argument lam is the value of \(\lambda\). So this probability is computed to be

Code 
dpois(15, 20)
[1] 0.05164885

The R function ppois() will compute cumulative probabilities, that is probabilities of particular values or smaller. For example, suppose we are interested in the probability that 12 or fewer arrive in the time interval. This is equal to

Code 
ppois(12, 20)
[1] 0.03901199

What is the probability that the number of arrivals falls between 12 and 22 inclusive? This is found by subtracting the probability of 11 or fewer from the probability of 22 or fewer.

Code 
ppois(22, 20) - ppois(11, 20)
[1] 0.6992245

For a Poisson distribution with rate \(\lambda\), the mean and variance values are given by \[ E(X) = \lambda, \, Var(X) = \lambda. \] In our ER example, by the Poisson approximation, we can compute the standard deviation of the number of patients arriving: \[ \sigma = \sqrt{20} = 4.47. \]

2.3 Continuous Distributions

2.3.1 Introduction

Consider a spinner experiment where the location of the spinner \(X\) can be any value between 0 and 100. Let’s try spinning 10,000 times on a computer. One way to summarize the spinner values is to divide the range into bins and count the number of values in each bin. A histogram is a display of the table of bin counts. Figure 2.2 displays four histograms where the numbers of bins are equal to 5, 10, 20 and 50.

Although there is variation in the bar heights, the general shape of each histogram seems to be pretty flat or uniform over the entire interval of possible values of \(X\) between 0 and 100. If we are able to spin the spinner a large number of times and use a large number of bins, the shape of the bin heights would look close to the Uniform density shown in Figure 2.3.

When the random variable \(X\) is continuous, such as the case of the spinner result here, then probabilities are represented by means of a smooth curve called a density curve. When \(X\) is continuous, then probabilities of intervals of \(X\) values are computed by areas under the density curve.

As a simple example, what is the chance that the spinner result falls between 0 and 100? Since the scale of the spinner is from 0 to 100, all spins must fall in this interval and the probability of \(X\) landing in (0, 100) is 1. This probability is represented by the total area under the horizontal line between 0 and 100. Since the area of this rectangle is given by height times base, and the base is equal to 100, the height of this density curve must be 1/100 = 0.01. In this case, the spinner result is said to have a Uniform distribution on the interval from 0 to 100, and the curve is a Uniform density on (0, 100).

By means of similar area computations, we find other probabilities about the spinner location \(X\).

  1. What is the probability the spin falls between 20 and 60? That is, what is \[ P(20 < X < 60)? \] This probability is equal to the shaded area under the Uniform density between 20 and 60. See Figure 2.4 Using again the formula for the area of a rectangle, the base is \(60 - 20 = 40\) and the height is 0.01, so \[ P(20 < X < 60) = 40 (0.01) = 0.4. \]
  1. What is the probability the spin is greater than 80? That is, what is \[P(X > 80)\] Figure 2.5 shows the area that needs to be computed to find this probability. Note that the area under the curve only between the values 80 and 100 is shaded, since \(X\) cannot be larger than 100. Again by finding the area of the shaded rectangle, we see that \(P(X > 80)\) = 20 (0.01) = 0.2.

2.3.2 Probability Density Function

Consider a random experiment where a continuous random variable \(X\) is observed such as the location of the spinner in Section \(\ref{sec:Uniform}\). Define the support of \(X\) to be the set of possible values for \(X\). For example, the support of \(X\) for the spinner example is the interval (0, 100). To describe probabilities about \(X\), a probability density function (pdf) denoted by \(f(x)\) is defined. Any function \(f\) will not work – we require that \(f\) satisfy two properties:

Property 1. The probability density function \(f\) must be nonnegative which means that \[\begin{equation} f(x) \ge 0, { for\, all\,} x. \end{equation}\]

Property 2. The total area under the probability density density \(f\) must be equal to 1. Mathematically, \[\begin{equation} \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) dx = 1. \end{equation}\]

2.3.3 Waiting for a Bus

To illustrate a probability density function, suppose that a professor has a class that meets three times a week. To get to class, the professor walks to a bus stop and wait for a bus to go to school. From past experience, the professor knows that she can wait any time between 0 and 10 minutes for the bus, and she knows that each waiting time between 0 and 10 minutes is equally likely. For a given week, what’s the chance that her longest wait will be under 7 minutes?

Let \(W\) denote her longest waiting time for the week. In thinking about plausible values of \(W\), it is clear that \(W\) ranges between 0 and 10 minutes and large values of \(W\) are more likely. A probability density function for \(W\) that reflects these beliefs is given by \[ f(w) = \frac{3w^2}{1000}, 0 < w < 10. \] This pdf for this longest waiting time is shown in Figure 2.6.

Before we go any further, we should check if this is indeed a legitimate probability density function:

  1. Note from the graph that the density does not take on negative values, so the first property is satisfied.
  2. Second, for it to be a probability density function, the entire area under the curve must be equal to 1. One can check this by finding the integral of the density between 0 and 10 (the region where the density if positive): \[ \int_0^{10} \frac{3w^2}{1000} dw = \frac{w^3}{1000}\Big|^{10}_0 = \frac{10^3}{1000} - \frac{0^3}{1000} = 1. \]

The entire area under the curve is indeed equal to 1, so \(f\) is a legitimate probability density function. Now that \(f\) is known to be a probability density function, we can use it to find probabilities. To find the probability that this longest waiting time is less than 7 minutes, \(P(W < 7)\), we wishe to compute the area under the density curve between 0 and 7, as shown in Figure 2.7.

This is equivalent to the integral \[ P(0 < W < 7) = \int_0^{7} \frac{3w^2}{1000} dw \] and, by evaluating this, we obtain the probability \[ P(0 < W < 7) = \int_0^{7} \frac{3w^2}{1000} dw = \frac{w^3}{1000}\Big|^{7}_0 = \frac{7^3}{1000} - \frac{0^3}{1000} = 0.343. \]

Suppose we are interested in the probability that the longest waiting time is between 6 and 8 minutes. This is represented by the shaded area in Figure 2.8.

To compute this area, we find the integral of the density between 6 and 8: \[ P(6 < W < 8) = \int_6^{8} \frac{3w^2}{1000} dw = \frac{w^3}{1000}\Big|^{8}_6 = \frac{8^3}{1000} - \frac{6^3}{1000} = 0.296. \]

Since a continuous random variable assigns zero probability to single values, this probability is not changed if we include the endpoints of the interval:

\[ P(6 \le W \le 8) = P(6 < W < 8) = 0.296. \]

2.3.4 The Cumulative Distribution Function

Consider an arbitrary random variable \(X\) with pdf \(f(x)\). Choose an arbitrary point \(x\) – the cumulative distribution function at \(x\), or cdf for short, is the probability that \(X\) is less than or equal to \(x\): \[\begin{equation} F(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(x) dx. \end{equation}\]

Consider the random variable \(W\), the longest waiting time in the bus waiting example. Suppose we choose a value of \(x\) in the interval (0, 10). Then \(F(x)\) would be the area under the density curve between 0 and \(x\) shown in Figure 2.9.

Writing this area as an integral, we compute \(F(x)\) as \[ F(x) = P(W \le x) = \int_0^x \frac{3w^2}{1000} dw = \frac{w^3}{1000}\Big|^{x}_0 = \frac{x^3}{1000}. \] This formula is valid for any value of \(x\) in the interval (0, 10).

In fact, \(F(x)\) is defined for all values of \(x\) on the real line.

  • If \(x\) is a value smaller or equal to 0, then we see from the figure that the probability that \(W\) is smaller than \(x\) is equal to 0. So \(F(x) = 0\) for \(x \le 0\).

  • On the other hand, if \(x\) is greater or equal to 10, then the probability that \(W\) is smaller than \(x\) is 1. So \(F(x) = 1\) for \(x \ge 10\).

Putting it all together, we see that the cdf \(F\) is given by \[ F(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x \le 0 \\ x^3 / 1000, & 0 < x < 10 \\ 1, & x \ge 10. \end{cases} \]

Once we have computed the cdf \(F\), probabilities are found by evaluating \(F\) at different points. For example, to find the probability that the maximum waiting time \(W\) is less than equal to 6 minutes, we just compute \(F(6) = P(W \le 6) = 6^3 / 1000 = 0.216\). Figure 2.10 displays the cdf function and illustrates the computation.

To compute the probability that the maximum waiting time exceeds 8 minutes, first note that “exceeding 8 minutes” is the complement event to “less than or equal to 8 minutes”, and so \[ P(W > 8) = 1 - P(W \le 8) = 1 - F(8) = 1 - \frac{8^3}{1000} = 0.488. \] Likewise, if we are interested in the chance that the waiting time \(W\) falls between 2 and 4, represent the probability as the difference of two “less-than” probabilities, and then subtract the two values of F. \[ P(2 < W < 4) = P(W \le 4) - P(W \le 2) = F(4) - F(2) = \frac{4^3}{1000} - \frac{2^3}{1000} = 0.056. \]

2.3.5 Mean, Standard Deviation and Percentile of a Continuous Random Variable

One is interested in summarizing a continuous random variable. Natural summaries are given by the mean \(\mu\) and the standard deviation \(\sigma\), where these quantities are defined in a similar manner as for a discrete random variable, with the exception that summations are replaced by integrals.

The mean \(\mu\), or equivalently the expected value of \(X\), is given by \[\begin{equation} \mu = E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x f(x) dx. \end{equation}\] Just as in the discrete random variable case, there is an attractive interpretation of \(\mu\). If we are able to observe a large number of values of \(X\), then \(\mu\) will be approximately equal to the sample mean \(\bar X\) of these random values of \(X\).

To define the spread of the values of \(X\), we first compute the average squared deviation about the mean, the variance, \[\begin{equation} \sigma^2 = Var(X) = E(X - \mu)^2 = \int_{-\infty}^\infty (x - \mu)^2 f(x) dx. \end{equation}\] The standard deviation of \(X\), \(\sigma\), is defined to be the square root of the variance.

Let’s illustrate the computation of the mean and standard deviation for the bus waiting time problem. Using the definition of \(f\), we get that the mean is equal to \[ \mu = \int_0^{10} x \left(\frac{3x^2}{1000}\right) dx. \] Performing the integration, we obtain \[ \mu = \int_0^{10} x \left(\frac{3x^2}{1000}\right) dx = \frac{3 x^4}{4000} \Big|^{10}_0 = \frac{3 (10)^4}{1000} = 7.5. \] On, the average, we expect the longest wait in a week to be 7.5 minutes.

The computation of the variance is a bit more tedious, but straightforward.
\[ \sigma^2 = \int_0^{10} (x -\mu)^2 \left(\frac{3x^2}{1000}\right) dx = 3.75. \] So the standard deviation of \(X\) is \(\sigma = \sqrt{3.75} = 1.94\).

Another useful summary of a continuous random variable is a percentile. The 70th percentile, for example, is the value of \(X\), call it \(x\), such that 70% of the probability is to the left, shown in Figure 2.11. That is, the 70th percentile, call it \(x_{70}\), satisfies the equation \[ P(X \le x_{70}) = 0.70. \]

Since we recognize the left hand side of the equation as equivalent to the cdf \(F\) (which already has been computed as \(x^3/1000\)), the equation is written as \[ F(x_{70}) = 0.70, \] that is, \[ \frac{x_{70}^3}{1000} = 0.70. \] To find the 70th percentile, the above equation is solved for \(x_{70}\) – after some algebra, we get \[ x_{70} = \sqrt[3]{700} = 8.88. \] This means that if we wait many weeks for this bus, approximately 70% of the longest waiting times will be shorter than 8.88 minutes.

2.3.6 Normal Distribution

One of the most popular races in the United States is the marathon, a grueling 26-mile run. Here we explore completion times for 520 people running the marathon. A histogram of these times, measured in minutes, is shown in Figure 2.12. The vertical scale is density so the areas the bars correspond to relative frequencies of the different time intervals.

Note that these measured times have similar to a bell shape. Figure 2.13 superimposes a Normal curve on top of this histogram. Note that this curve is a reasonable match to the histogram. In fact, data like this marathon time data that are measurements are often well approximated by a Normal curve.

A Normal density curve has the general form \[\begin{equation} f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi} \sigma} \exp\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right\}, -\infty < x < \infty. \end{equation}\] This density curve is described by two parameters – the mean \(\mu\) and the standard deviation \(\sigma\). The mean \(\mu\) is the center of the curve. The mean of the Normal curve fit to the marathon time data is \(\mu\) = 229. The number \(\sigma\), the standard deviation, describes the spread of the curve. Here the Normal curve standard deviation is \(\sigma\) = 47. If we know the mean and standard deviation of the Normal curve, we can make reasonable predictions where the majority of times of the women runners will fall.

Suppose that the Normal density with \(\mu\) = 229 minutes and \(\sigma\)= 47 minutes represents the distribution of racing times. Say we are interested in the probability that a runner completes the race less than 4 hours or 240 minutes. One computes this probability by finding an area under the Normal curve. Specifically, as indicated in Figure 2.14, this probability is the area under the curve for all times less than 240 minutes.

2.3.7 Binomial Probabilities and the Normal Curve

The Normal curve is useful for modeling batches of data, especially when we are collecting measurements of some process. But actually there is a more important justification for the Normal curve. We will explore several important results about the pattern of Binomial probabilities and sample means and we will find these results useful in our introduction to statistical inference in later chapters.

First, consider different shapes of Binomial distributions. Suppose that half of the student body lives on-campus and a sample survey of \(n\) students is conduct to learn if the study body is interested in using a new recreational sports complex that is proposed. Let \(X\) denote the number of on-campus students in the sample. Assuming a random sample is chosen, it is known that \(X\) will be distributed Binomial with parameters \(n\) and \(p=0.5\). What is the shape of the Binomial probabilities? Figure 2.19 displays the Binomial probabilities for sample sizes \(n\) = 10, 20, 50, and 100.

What does we notice about these probability graphs? First, note that each distribution is symmetric about the mean \(\mu = n p\). But more interesting, the shape of the distribution seems to resemble a Normal curve as the number of trials \(n\) increases.

Perhaps this pattern happens since we started with a Binomial distribution with \(p\) = 0.5 and we would not see this behavior if a different value of \(p\) was used. Suppose that only 10% of all students would use the new facility and let \(X\) denote the number of students in your sample who say they would use the facility. The random variable \(X\) would be distributed Binomial with parameters \(n\) and \(p\) = 0.1. Figure 2.20 shows the probability distributions again for the sample sizes \(n\) = 10, 20, 50, and 100. As we might expect the shape of the probabilities for \(n=10\) are not very Normal-shaped – the distribution is skewed right. But note that as \(n\) increases, the probabilities become more Normal-shaped and the Normal curve seems to be a good match for \(n=100\).

Figure 2.19 and Figure 2.20 illustrate a basic result: if we consider a Binomial random variable \(X\) with \(n\) trials and probability of success \(p\), then, as the number of trials \(n\) approaches infinity, the distribution of the standardized score \[\begin{equation} Z = \frac{X - n p}{\sqrt{n p (1 - p)}} \end{equation}\] approaches a standard Normal random variable, that is a Normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. This is a very useful result. It means, that for a large number of trials, we can approximate a Binomial random variable \(X\) by a Normal random variable with mean and standard deviation \[\begin{equation} \mu = n p, \, \, \, \sigma = \sqrt{n p (1 - p)}. \end{equation}\]

This approximation result can be illustrated with our student survey example. Suppose that 10% of the student body would use the new recreational sports complex. One takes a random sample of 100 students — what’s the probability that 5 or fewer students in the sample would use the new facility?

The random variable \(X\) in this problem is the number of students in the sample that would use the facility. This random variable has a Binomial distribution with \(n = 100\) and \(p = 0.1\) that is pictured as a histogram in Figure 2.21. By the approximation result, this distribution is approximated by a Normal curve with \(\mu = 100 (0.1) = 10\) and \(\sigma = \sqrt{100 (0.1) (0.9)} = 3\) This Normal curve is placed on top of the probability histogram in Figure 2.21 – note that it is a pretty good fit to the histogram.

One is interested in the probability that at most 5 students use the facility, that is, \(P(X \le 5)\). This probability is approximated by the area under a Normal(10, 3) curve between \(X=0\) and \(X=5\). Using the R function, we compute this Normal curve area to be

Code

[1] 0.04736129

In this case, we can also find this probability exactly by a calculator or computer program that computes Binomial probabilities. Using the function in R, we find the probability that \(X\) is at most 5 is

Code 
pbinom(5, size = 100, prob = 0.10)
[1] 0.05757689

Here we see that the Normal approximation gives a similar answer to the exact Binomial computation.

2.4 Joint Probability Mass Function

Up to this point, we have been considers experiments where only one random variable is observed. But certainly, it is common to collect several random variables in experiments. For example, in a running race, we may collect the identity and time for the winning runner. We want to extend our description of probabilities to situations when two variables are observed.

To begin the discussion of two random variables, we start with an example of balls in a box. Consider a box of ten balls – four are white, three are red, and three are black. Five balls are selected out of the box without replacement and the number of white and red balls in the sample are counted. What is the probability two white and two red balls are observed in the sample?

  1. First, we think about the total number of ways of selecting five balls with replacement from a box of ten balls. One assumes the balls are distinct and the order that of the ball selection does not matter. Then the total number of outcomes is \[ N = {10 \choose 5} = 252. \]

  2. Next, we think about the number of ways of selecting two white and two red balls. One does this in steps – first select the white balls, then select the red balls, and then select the one remaining black ball. Note that five balls are selected, so exactly one of the balls must be black. Since the box has four white balls, the number of ways of choose two white is \({4 \choose 2} = 6\). Of the three red balls, we want to choose two – the number of ways of doing that is \({3 \choose 2} = 3\). Last, the number of ways of choosing the remaining one black ball is \({3 \choose 1} = 3.\) So the total number of ways of choosing two white, two red, and one black ball is the product \[ {4 \choose 2} \times {3 \choose 2} \times {3 \choose 1} = 6 \times 3 \times 3 = 54. \]

  3. Each one of the \({10 \choose 5} = 252\) possible outcomes of five balls is equally likely to be chosen. Of these outcomes, 54 resulted in two white and two red balls, so the probability of choosing two white and two red balls is \[ P({2 \, white \, and \, 2 \, red}) = \frac{54}{252}. \]

Here the probability of choosing a specific number of white and red balls has been found. To do this calculation for other outcomes, it is convenient to define two random variables

\(X\) = number of red balls selected, \(Y\) = number of white balls selected.

Based on what was found, \[ P(X = 2, Y = 2) = \frac{54}{252}. \]

Joint Probability Mass Function

Suppose this calculation is done for every possible pair of values of \(X\) and \(Y\). The table of probabilities is given in Table 2.2.

This table is called the joint probability mass function (pmf) \(f(x, y)\) of (\(X, Y\)).
As for any probability distribution, we require that each of the probability values are nonnegative and the sum of the probabilities over all values of \(X\) and \(Y\) is one. That is, the function \(f(x, y)\) satisfies two properties:

  1. \(f(x, y) \ge 0\), for all \(x\), \(y\)
  2. \(\Sigma_{x, y} f(x, y) = 1\)

It is clear from Table 2.2 that all of the probabilities are nonnegative and the reader can confirm that the sum of the probabilities is equal to one.

Using Table 2.2, we see that some particular pairs \((x, y)\) are not possible as \(f(x, y) = 0\). For example, \(f(0, 1) = 0\) which means that it is not possible to observe 0 red balls and 1 white ball in the sample. Note that five balls were sampled, and if we only observed one red or white ball, that means that we must have sampled \(5 - 1 = 4\) black balls which is not possible.

We find probabilities of any event involving \(X\) and \(Y\) by summing probabilities from Table 2.2.

  • What is\(P(X = Y)\), the probability that we sample the same number of red and white balls? By the table, we see that this is possible only when \(X = 1, Y = 1\) or \(X = 2, Y = 2\). So the probability \[ P(X = Y) = f(1, 1) + f(2, 2) = \frac{12}{252} + \frac{54}{252} = \frac{66}{252}. \]
  • What is \(P(X > Y)\), the probability we sample more red balls than white balls? From the table, we identify the outcomes where \(X > Y\), and then sum the corresponding probabilities. \[\begin{align*} P(X > Y) & = f(1, 0) + f(2, 0) + f(2, 1) + f(3, 0) + f(3, 1) + f(3, 2) \\ & = \frac{12}{252} + \frac{3}{252} + \frac{36}{252} + \frac{3}{252} + \frac{12}{252} + \frac{6}{252} \\ & = \frac{72}{252} \end{align*}\]

2.5 Joint Probability Density Functions

One can also describe probabilities when the two variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are continuous. As a simple example, suppose that we randomly choose two points \(X\) and \(Y\) on the interval (0, 2) where \(X < Y\). One defines the joint probability density function or joint pdf of \(X\) and \(Y\) to be the function \[ f(x, y) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}, \, \, 0 < x < y <2;\\ 0, \, \,{elsewhere}. \end{cases} \]

This joint pdf is viewed as a plane of constant height over the set of points \((x, y)\) where \(0 < x < y <2\). This region of points in the plane is shown in Figure 2.22.

In the one variable situation in Section 2.3, a function \(f\) is a legitimate density function or pdf if it is nonnegative over the real line and the total area under the curve is equal to one. Similarly for two variables, any function \(f(x, y)\) is considered a pdf if it satisfies two properties:

  1. The density is nonnegative over the whole plane: \[\begin{equation} f(x, y) \ge 0, \, \, {for} \, {all} \, \, x, y. \end{equation}\]
  2. The total volume under the density is equal to one: \[\begin{equation} \int \int f(x, y) dx dy = 1. \end{equation}\]

The density in our example is indeed a legitimate joint pdf. It is pretty obvious that the density that was defined is nonnegative, but it is less clear that the integral of the density is equal to one. Since the density is a plane of constant height, we compute this double integral geometrically. Using the familiar ``one half base times height” argument, the area of the triangle in the plane is \((1/2) (2) (2) = 2\) and since the pdf has constant height of \(1/2\), the volume under the surface is equal to \(2 (1/2) =1\). One can also compute the double integral as follows to confirm that the total volume is equal to one. \[\begin{align*} \int_{0}^2 \int^y_{3-y} f(x, y) dx dy & = \int_{0}^2 \int^y_{3-y} \frac{1}{2} dx dy \\ & = \int_{0}^2 \frac{2 y - 3}{2}dy \\ & = \frac{y^2 - 3 y}{ 2} \Big|_{0}^2 \\ & = \frac{2^3 - 3 (2)}{2} = 1. \end{align*}\]

Probabilities about \(X\) and \(Y\) are found by finding volumes under the pdf surface. For example, suppose we wish to find the probability that the sum of locations \(X + Y > 3\), that is \(P(X + Y > 3)\). The region in the \((x, y)\) plane of interest is first identified, and then we find the volume under the joint pdf over this region.

In Figure 2.23, the region where \(x + y > 3\) has been shaded. The probability \(P(X + Y > 3)\) is the volume under the joint pdf over this region. Applying a geometric argument, we note that the area of the shaded region is \(1/4\), and so the probability of interest is \((1/4)(1/2) = 1/8\). One also finds this probability by integrating the joint pdf over the region as follows: \[\begin{align*} P(X + Y < 3) & = \int_{1.5}^2 \int^y_{3-y} f(x, y) dx dy \\ & = \int_{1.5}^2 \int^y_{3-y} \frac{1}{2} dx dy \\ & = \int_{1.5}^2 \frac{2 y - 3}{2}dy \\ & = \frac{y^2 - 3 y}{ 2} \Big|_{1.5}^2 \\ & = \frac{1}{8}. \end{align*}\]

2.6 Marginal Probability Functions

2.6.1 Discrete Random Variables

Once a joint probability mass function for \((X, Y)\) has been constructed, we find probabilities for one of the two variables. In our balls-in-box example in Section 2.4, suppose we want to find the probability that exactly three red balls are chosen, that is \(P(X = 3)\). This probability is found by summing values of the joint pmf \(f(x, y)\) where \(x = 3\) and \(y\) can be any possible value of the random variable \(Y\), that is, \[\begin{align*} P(X = 3) & = \sum_y f(3, y) \\ & = f(3, 0) + f(3, 1) + f(3, 2) \\ & = \frac{3}{252} + \frac{12}{252} + \frac{6}{252} \\ & = \frac{21}{252}. \end{align*}\]

This operation is done for each of the possible values of \(X\) – the marginal probability mass function of \(X\), \(f_X()\) is defined as follows: \[\begin{equation} f_X(x) = \sum_y f(x, y). \end{equation}\] One finds this marginal pmf of \(X\) from Table 2.2 by summing the joint probabilities for each row of the table. The marginal pmf of \(X\) is displayed in Table 2.3.

Note that a marginal pmf is a legitimate probability function in that the values are nonnegative and the probabilities sum to one.

We can also find the marginal pmf of \(Y\), denoted by \(f_Y()\), by a similar operation – for a fixed value of \(Y = y\) we sum over all of the possible values of \(X\). \[\begin{equation} f_Y(y) = \sum_x f(x, y). \end{equation}\]

For example, if we want to find \(f_Y(2) = P(Y = 2)\) in our example, we sum the joint probabilities in Table 2.2 over the rows in the column where \(Y = 2\). One obtains the probability: \[\begin{align*} f_Y(2) & = \sum_x f(x, 2) \\ & = f(0, 2) + f(1, 2) + f(2, 2) + f(3, 2)\\ & = \frac{6}{252} + \frac{54}{252} + \frac{54}{252} + \frac{6}{252}\\ & = \frac{120}{252}. \end{align*}\] By repeating this exercise for each value of \(Y\), we obtain the marginal pmf of \(Y\) displayed in Table 2.4.

2.6.2 Continuous Random Variables

Given a joint pdf \(f(x, y)\) that describes probabilities of two continuous variables \(X\) and \(Y\), we summarize probabilities about each variable individually by the computation of marginal pdfs. The marginal pdf of \(X\), \(f_X(x)\), is obtained by integrating out \(y\) from the joint pdf. \[\begin{equation} f_X(x) = \int f(x, y) dy. \end{equation}\] In a similar fashion, we define the marginal pdf of \(Y\) by integrating out \(x\) from the joint pdf. \[\begin{equation} f_Y(x) = \int f(x, y) dx. \end{equation}\]

Let’s illustrate the computation of marginal pdfs for the example of Section 2.5. One has to be careful about the limits of the integration due to the dependence between \(x\) and \(y\) in the support of the joint density. Looking back at Figure 2.22, we see that if the value of \(x\) is fixed, then the limits for \(y\) go from \(x\) to 2. So the marginal density of \(X\) is given by \[\begin{align*} f_X(x) & = \int f(x, y) dy \\ & = \int_x^2 \frac{1}{2} dy \\ & = \frac{2 - x}{2}, \, \, 0 < x < 2. \end{align*}\] By a similar calculation, we can verify that the marginal density of \(Y\) is equal to \[ f_Y(y) = \frac{y}{2}, \, \, 0 < y < 2. \]

2.7 Conditional Probability Functions

2.7.1 Discrete Random Variables

In the sampling balls from a box example from Section 2.4 and Section 2.6.1 , suppose we are told that exactly two red balls are sampled, that is \(X\) = 2 – how does that information change the probabilities about the number of white balls \(Y\)?

In this example, we are interested in finding \(P(Y = y \mid X = 2)\). Using the definition of conditional probability, we have \[\begin{align*} P(Y = y \mid X = 2) & = \frac{P(Y = y, X = 2)}{P(X = 2)}. \\ & = \frac{f(2, y)}{f_X(2)} \end{align*}\] For example, the probability of observing two white balls given that we have two red balls is equal to \[\begin{align*} P(Y = 2 \mid X = 2) & = \frac{P(Y = 2, X = 2)}{P(X = 2)} \\ & = \frac{f(2, 2)}{f_X(2)} \\ & = \frac{54 / 252}{105/252} = \frac{54}{105}. \\ \end{align*}\]

Suppose this calculation is repeated for all possible values of \(Y\) – we obtain the values displayed in Table 2.5.

These probabilities represent the conditional probability mass function for \(Y\) conditional on \(X = 2\). This conditional pmf is just like any other probability distribution in that the values are nonnegative and they sum to one. To illustrate using this distribution, suppose we are told that two red balls are selected (that is, \(X = 2\)) and we want to find the probability that more than one white ball is chosen. This probability is given by \[\begin{align*} P(Y > 1 \mid X = 2) & = \Sigma_{y >1} \, \, f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid X = 2) \\ & = f_{Y \mid X}(2 \mid X = 2) + f_{Y \mid X}(3 \mid X = 2) \\ & = \frac{54}{105} + \frac{12}{105} = \frac{66}{105}. \end{align*}\]

In general, the conditional pmf of \(Y\) conditional on \(X = x\), denoted by \(f_{Y\mid X}(y \mid x)\), is defined to be \[\begin{equation} f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{f(x, y)}{f_X(x)}, \, \, { if} \, \, f_X(x) > 0. \end{equation}\]

2.7.2 Continuous Random Variables

One can also define conditional probability densities in the continuous random variable. Returning to our choose two points example in Section 2.5 and 2.6.2, suppose we are told that the first random location is equal to \(X = 1.5\). What have we learned about the value of the second random variable \(Y\)?

To answer this question, we define the notion of a conditional pdf. The conditional pdf of the random variable \(Y\) given the value \(X = x\) is defined as the quotient \[\begin{equation} f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid X = x) = \frac{f(x, y)}{f_X(x)}, \, \, { if} \, \, f_X(x) > 0. \end{equation}\] In our example we are given that \(X = 1.5\). Looking at Figure 2.22, we see that when \(X = 1.5\), the only possible values of \(Y\) are between 1.5 and 2. By substituting the values of \(f(x, y)\) and \(f_X(x)\), we obtain \[\begin{align*} f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid X = 1.5) & = \frac{f(1.5, y)}{f_X(1.5)} \\ & = \frac{1/2}{(2 - 1.5) / 2} \\ & = 2, \, \, 1.5 < y < 2. \\ \end{align*}\] In other words, the conditional density for \(Y\) when \(X = 1.5\) is uniform from 1.5 to 2.

A conditional pdf is a legitimate density function, so the integral of the conditional pdf of \(Y\) given a value of \(X\) over all values \(y\) is equal to one. We can use this density to compute conditional probabilities. For example, if \(X\) = 1.5, what is the probability that \(Y\) is greater than 1.7? This probability is the conditional probability \(P(Y > 1.7 \mid X = 1.5)\) that is equal to an integral over the conditional density \(f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid 1.5)\): \[\begin{align*} P(Y > 1.7 \mid X = 1.5) & = \int_{1.7}^2 f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid 1.5) dy \\ & = \int_{1.7}^2 2 dy \\ & = 0.6. \\ \end{align*}\]

Turn the Random Variables Around

Above, we looked at the pdf of \(Y\) conditional on a value of \(X\). One can also consider a pdf of \(X\) conditional on a value of \(Y\). Returning to our example, suppose that we learn that \(Y\), the larger random variable on the interval is equal to 0.8. In this case, what would we expect for the random variable \(X\)?

This question can be answered in two steps – we first find the conditional pdf of \(X\) conditional on \(Y = 0.8\). Then once this conditional pdf is found, we find the mean of this distribution.

The conditional pdf of \(X\) given the value \(Y = y\) is defined as the quotient \[\begin{equation} f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid Y = y) = \frac{f(x, y)}{f_Y(y)}, \, \, { if} \, \, f_Y(y) > 0. \end{equation}\] Looking back at Figure 2.22, we see that if \(Y = 0.8\), the possible values of \(X\) are from 0 to 0.8. Over these values the conditional pdf of \(X\) is given by \[\begin{align*} f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid Y = 0.8) & = \frac{f(x, 0.8)}{f_Y(0.8)} \\ & = \frac{1/2}{0.8 / 2} \\ & = 1.25, \, \, 0 < x < 0.8. \\ \end{align*}\] So if we know that \(Y = 0.8\), then the conditional pdf for \(X\) is Uniform on (0, 0.8).

To find the expected value of \(X\) knowing that \(Y = 0.8\), we find the mean of this distribution. \[\begin{align*} E(X \mid Y = 0.8) & = \int_0^{0.8} x f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid 0.8) dx \\ & = \int_0^{0.8} x \,1.25 \, dx \\ \\ & = (0.8)^2 / 2 \times 1.25 = 0.4. \\ \end{align*}\]

# Probability Distributions 

## Introduction

The goal of this chapter is to introduce probability by covering several common probability distributions.  A random variable is a number assigned to the outcome of a experiment where the outcome is not known in advance.  In the case where the random variable can only assume a countable number of values, the associated probabilities can described by a discrete probability distribution.  @sec-discrete introduces the construction of probabilities for a discrete random variable and introduces the Binomial and Negative Binomial distributions which are based on a coin-tossing experiment.

In the situation where the random variable takes on a continuum of values, probabilities are represented as areas under a density curve.  @sec-continuous introduces continuous random variables by use of spinner and waiting time examples.  This section introduces the Normal density which is a common function for representing a measurement random variable.

The following sections describe joint probability distributions for two random variables.  @sec-jointpmf describes  probability distributions for two discrete random variables and @sec-jointpdf describes the use of a density curve to represent probabilities for two continuous random variables.  In this setting, @sec-marginal discusses how these joint probability distributions can be used to extract marginal probability distributions for a single variable.  @sec-conditional describes the construction of probability distributions for one variable conditional on a value of the second variable.


## Discrete Probability Distributions{#sec-discrete}

### A Simple Coin Game

Suppose that Peter and Paul play a simple coin game.  A coin is tossed.  If the coin lands heads, then Peter receives \$2 from Paul; otherwise Peter has to pay \$2 to Paul.  The game is played for a total of five coin flips.
After the five flips, what is Peter's net gain (in dollars)?

This is an illustration of a _random experiment_ where the outcome of the flips is unknown.  For each possible outcome of the flips, say $HTHHT$, there will be a corresponding net gain for Peter.  For this outcome, Peter won three times and lost twice, so his net gain is $3(2) - 2(2) = 2$ dollars.   The net gain is an example of a  _random variable_ -- a number  assigned to each outcome of the random experiment.  Generally, a capital letter will be used to represent a random variable -- here the capital letter $G$ denotes Peter's gain in this experiment.  

The next task is to assign probabilities for the values of the random variable. There are $2 ^ 5 = 32$ possible outcomes of this experiment and since these outcomes are equally likely, we assign to each outcome a probability of 1/32.  We can compute the probability of a value for $G$ by counting the number of outcomes corresponding to that value and divide the number by 32.  For example, the five outcomes where Peter's net gain is $G = 6$ are

HHHHT, HHHTH, HHTHH, HTHHH, THHHH

and so $P(G = 6)$ is 5/32.

If we enumerate these 32 outcomes and calculate the associated net gain value for each outcome, we see there are 6 possible values of $G$: -10, -6, -2, 2, 6, and 10 dollars. By counting the number of outcomes for each value of $G$ and dividing the count by 32, we can compute the probability of all values.  If we list the values of $G$ together with the associated probabilities, we obtain a _probability distribution_ for $G$ as displayed in @tbl-peterpaul.

### Discrete Random Variable

In general, $X$ is a _discrete random variable_ if it only assigns probability to a discrete set of values.   The function $f(x)$ is a _probability mass function_ (pmf) for $X$ if the function satisfies two properties.

-  $f(x) \ge 0$ for each possible value $x$ of $X$
- $\sum_x f(x) = 1$ (the probabilities sum to 1 over all values of $X$)

A _discrete probability distribution_ is a listing of the values of $X$ together with the associated values of the pmf.  We graphically displays this probability distribution with a bar graph\index{bar graph}.  All of the values of $G$ are placed on the horizontal axis, a probability scale is marked on the vertical scale, and then vertical lines are drawn on the graph corresponding to the pmf values. @fig-peterpaul displays the graph of the probability distribution of $G$ in this example.


### Mean and Standard Deviation of a Discrete Random Variable

A probability distribution of a discrete random variable can be used to find probabilities of interest. For example, what is the probability Peter wins money in this game?  Peter wins money if the gain $G$ is positive and this corresponds to the values $G = 2, 6, 10$.  By adding up the probabilities of these three values, we see the probability that Peter wins money is

\begin{align*}
P({ Peter \, \, wins}) &= P(G > 0) \\
&= P(G = 2) + P(G = 6) + P(G = 10) \\
&= \frac{10 + 5+1}{32} = \frac{1}{2}.
\end{align*}

A common measure of "average" of a probability distribution is the _mean_ or _expected value_ of $X$, denoted $\mu$ or $E(X)$. The mean (or expected value) is found by weighting each value of $X$ by its associated probability and summing over all values of $X$:

\begin{equation}
 \mu = \sum_x x  f(x).
\end{equation} 
 
Two measures of spread of a random variable $X$ are the variance, denoted by $\sigma^2$, and the standard deviation denoted by  $\sigma$.  To compute the variance, we weight the values of $(x - \mu)^2$ by the associated probabilities of $X$ and find the sum of the products.
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2 = \Sigma_x (x - \mu)^2 f(x).
\end{equation}
The variance represents an "average" squared distance (or deviation) from the mean $\mu$.

The standard deviation is the square root of the variance:
\begin{align}
\sigma & =  \sqrt{\sigma^2} \nonumber \\
       & =  \sqrt{E(X - \mu)^2} \nonumber \\
       & =  \sqrt{\Sigma_x(x-\mu)^2f(x)}
\end{align}
The standard deviation is often more useful than the variance as $\sigma$ is measured on the same scale as the random variable $X$.

For the Peter-Paul game, the mean can be computed to be
$$
\mu = -10 (1/32) -6 (5/32) -2 (10/32) + 2 (10/32) + 6 (5/32) + 10 (1/32) = 0.
$$

 Note that $G = 0$ is not a possible outcome of the game -- generally the mean of a random variable does not have to be a possible outcome value.  But the mean or expected value has a long-run frequency interpretation. If Peter and Paul play this game a large number of times, then the value $\mu$= 0 represents the mean winnings of Peter in all of these games. 
 
By use of Equation (2.2), the variance is given by
 
\begin{align*}
\sigma^2 & = (-10 -0)^2 (1/32) +  (-6 - 0)^2 (5/32) + (-2 - 0)^2 (10/32) + \\
 & (2 - 0)^2 (10/32) + (6 - 0)^2 (5/32) + (10 - 0)^2 (1/32) = 20.
\end{align*}
and so the standard deviation is equal to
$$
\sigma =  \sqrt{20} = 4.47.
$$

The value 4.417 represents an average or typical deviation of Peter's gain (in dollars) from the mean value of 0.  Since the value of the standard deviation is relatively large, this indicates that Peter's actual gain in this game is uncertain, that is, there is much variability in Peter's gain in repeated plays of the game.

### Binomial Distribution

The Binomial distribution is one of the most commonly used distributions in Bayesian inference. It is often used as the data model for random experiments with two outcomes.

Consider the following random process called a Binomial experiment.
  
1. The same basic task or trial is repeated many times -- let the number of trials be denoted by $n$.
2. On each trial, there are two possible outcomes, which are called "success" or "failure". Examples of such outcomes include "yes" or "no", or "0" or "1". 
3. The probability of a success, denoted by $p$, is the same for each trial.
4. The results of outcomes from different trials are independent.  This means that the chance of a particular outcome does not depend on outcomes on previous trials.

We are generally interested in $X$, the number of observed successes out of $n$ trials in this experiment..
The probability of $X = x$ successes is given by the Binomial formula:
\begin{equation}
P(X = x) = {{n} \choose {x}} p^x (1 - p)^{n-x}, \, \, k = x ..., n,
\end{equation}
where ${n \choose x}$ is the choose function
\begin{equation}
{n \choose x} = \frac{n!}{x! (n - x)!}.
\end{equation}

Let's illustrate using this formula for a familiar task of throwing a six-sided die.  Suppose you roll a fair die five times and record the number of 4's that are observed. This can be considered a Binomial experiment where the number of trials $n = 5$ and the probability of success on a single trial is $p = 1/6$.  The random variable $X$ is the number of rolls of 4 during this game.

Using Equation (2.4), the probability that we observe exactly two 4's, that is $P(X = 2)$ is equal to
$$
P(X = 2) = {5 \choose 2} (1/6)^2 (1 - 1/6)^{5-2}.
$$
This computation in R can be directly performed by use of the `choose()` function.

```{r}
choose(5, 2) * (1/6) ^ 2 * (1 - 1/6) ^ (5 - 2)
```


Binomial probabilities can also be computed by use of the  R function `dbinom()` that calculates the pmf of a Binomial distribution for values of the parameters $n$ and $p$.  The inputs to `dbinom()` are the number of successes of interest $x$ and the `size` $n$ and `prob` $p$.

```{r}
dbinom(2, size = 5, prob = 1 / 6)
```


What is the probability that we roll at least one 4 in this experiment?  This computation can be done by using the `dbinom()` function to find the probabilities of the values $X$ = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and then using the `sum()` function to add up these individual probabilities.

```{r}
sum(dbinom(1:5, size = 5, prob = 1/6))
```

The chance of rolling at least one 4 in five rolls is 0.598.

The mean and variance of a Binomial random variable $X$ are given by
\begin{equation}
E(X) = n p, \, \, \, Var(X) = n p (1-p).
\end{equation}

In our dice rolling example  where $X$ denoted the number of 4's in five rolls,   the number of trials and probability of success are given by $n$ = 5 and $p$ = 1/6.  The expected number of 4's would be
$$
E(X) = 5 (1/6) = 0.833
$$
and the variance of the number of 4's would be
$$
Var(X) =5 (1/6) (1 - 1/6) = 25/36 = 0.694.
$$

When the Binomial experiment is repeated many times, the  mean and variance of $X$ can be approximated by the sample mean and sample variance of the simulated draws of the random variable.  In this example, we can simulate 5000 draws of five dice by use of the `rbinom()` function.  The vector `four_rolls` contains the number of 4's observed in these 5000 experiments. The `mean()` and `var()` functions are used to compute the sample mean and sample variance of these draws.  Note that the values are close approximations to exact the values of $E(X$) and $Var(X)$ from Equation (2.6).

```{r}
four_rolls <- rbinom(5000, size = 5, prob = 1/6)
c(M = mean(four_rolls), V = var(four_rolls))
```
This is an illustration of an approximation by  _Monte Carlo simulation_ that will be seen in later chapters to be an important Bayesian computation tool.

Although the Binomial distribution is popular for representing experiments with two outcomes for its ease in probability computations, its use depends on the assumptions of a Binomial experiment.  In cases where the probability of success is not constant across trials or where the trials are not independent, then alternative probability distributions should be used.


### Negative Binomial Distribution

In a Binomial experiment, it is assumed that the number of trials is fixed.  In alternative scenarios, we may be observing binary outcomes where the number of outcomes is actually random.  To illustrate this situation, consider a baseball example featuring a famous Japanese hitter.

In the 2004 baseball season Ichiro Suzuki of the Seattle Mariners had the opportunity to break the  record for the most hits in a single season.  George Sisler's record was 257 hits and Suzuki had 255 hits before the Mariners' game on September 30.  Was it likely that Suzuki would tie Sisler's record during this particular game?
  
 This process can be viewed as a coin-tossing experiment.  When Suzuki comes to bat, there are two relevant outcomes:  either he will get a hit, or he will get an out.  Assume the probability that he gets a hit on a single at-bat is $p$ = 0.372 (his current batting average) and the outcomes on different at-bats are  independent.  Suzuki needs two more hits to tie the record.  How many outs will it take him to get two hits?
  
  This is not a Binomial experiment since the number of trials is not fixed.  Instead the number of successes is fixed in advance and the number of failures to achieve this is random.  In this example, we are interested in probabilities about $X$ = number of outs (failures) to get two hits (successes). 

A general Negative Binomial experiment is described as follows:


1.  There is a sequence of independent trials where each trial can be a success or a failure.
2.  The probability of a success on a single trial is $p$.
3.  The experiment is continued until we observe $r$ successes, and $X$ = number of observed failures.

The probability that it takes $x$ failures to observe $r$ successes, the pmf of $X$, is given by
\begin{equation}
P(X = x) = {r + x -1 \choose r-1} p^r (1- p)^x, x = 0, 1, 2 ...
\end{equation}

Let's use R and this formula in our baseball example where $r = 2$ and $p$ = 0.372 and we wish to compute the probability $X = 2$:

```{r}
r <- 2
p <- .372
choose(r + 2 -1, r - 1) * p ^ r * (1 - p) ^ 2
```

The R function `dnbinom()` provides Negative Binomial probabilities where the first argument is `x`, `size` is the value of $r$ and `prob` is the value of $p$.  Using this function, the following table gives the probabilities for the number of outs values $x$ = 0, 1, 2, ..., 7.

```{r}
x <- 0:7
data.frame(x, prob = dnbinom(x, size = 2, prob = 0.372))
```


Note that it is most likely that Suzuki will get one out to get his two additional hits, but the probability of one out is only 17%.    There is a significant probability that Suzuki will take more outs. We use R to add the probabilities that $X$ is at most 7:

```{r}
sum(dnbinom(0:7, size = 2, prob = 0.372))
```

We see that the probability that $X$ is at most 7 is 0.904, so the probability that $X$ exceeds 7 is $1 - 0.904 = 0.096$. 

For a Negative Binomial experiment where $X$ is the number of failures needed to observe $r$ successes,  the mean and variance values are given by
\begin{equation}
 E(X) = \frac{r (1 - p)}{p}, Var(X) = \frac{r (1 - p)}{p^2}.
\end{equation}
For the baseball example, $r = 2$ and $p = 0.372$, so the expected number of outs to get two hits would be $E(X) = 2 (1 - 0.372) /0.372 = 3.4$.  Note that although $X = 1$ is the most probable value, Suzuki would average over 3 outs to get 2 hits in many repetitions of this random experiment.

Note that sometimes in a Negative Binomial experiment, the random variable is defined to be $Y$, the number of trials to observe $r$ successes.  The formula for the probability distribution of $Y$ differs from the one for $X$, but we can perform probability calculations with either definition.

### Poisson Distribution

Another discrete probability distribution useful in Bayesian settings is the Poisson distribution
Let the random variable $X$ denote the number of occurrences of an event in an interval with sample space\index{sample!space} $\{0, 1, 2, \cdots \}$. 
The variable $X$ follows a Poisson distribution with rate parameter $\lambda$ when the pmf of observing $x$ events in an interval is given by

\begin{eqnarray}
f(X = x \mid \lambda) = e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^x}{x!}, \, \, x = 0, 1, 2, ...
\end{eqnarray}
where $\lambda$ is the average number of events per interval, $e = 2.71828...$ is  Euler's number, and $x!$ is the factorial\index{factorial} of $x$.

The Poisson sampling model is based on the following assumptions about the sampling process:

1. It is assumed that the time interval is fixed.

2. Counts of arrivals occurring during different time intervals are independent.

3. The rate $\lambda$ at which the arrivals occur is constant over time.

To check the suitability of the Poisson distribution, we need to check these conditions for the particular application.

Consider am example where a hospital wants to determine how many doctors and nurses to assign on their emergency room (ER) team between 10pm and 11pm during the week. An important piece of information is the count of  patients arriving in the ER in this one-hour period. For this example, the event is a patient's arrival to the ER, and the fixed time interval is the period between 10pm and 11pm. 

In this ER example it is reasonable to assume that the time of one patient's arrival does not influence the time of another patient's arrival.  So it is assumed that events occur independently\index{independent} during different time intervals. In addition, we might not think that the rate of patient arrivals would change much through one hour during the evening, so it seems reasonable to assume that the average number of events is constant in the fixed interval. 

Suppose that 20 patients on average arrive in the ER in the one-hour period, so we set $\lambda = 20$. The random variable $X$ is the number of patients arriving in the interval.  What is the probability that exactly 15 patients arrive?  We apply the Poisson pmf in Equation (2.9):

$$
P(X = 15) = e^{-20} \frac{20^{15}}{15!}
$$
The R function `dpois()` computes Poisson probabilities where the first argument is `x` and the second argument `lam` is the value of $\lambda$.  So this probability is computed to be

```{r}
dpois(15, 20)
```

The R function `ppois()` will compute cumulative probabilities, that is probabilities of particular values or smaller.  For example, suppose we are interested in the probability that 12 or fewer arrive in the time interval.  This is equal to

```{r}
ppois(12, 20)
```
What is the probability that the number of arrivals falls between 12 and 22 inclusive?  This is found by subtracting the probability of 11 or fewer from the probability of 22 or fewer.

```{r}
ppois(22, 20) - ppois(11, 20)
```

For a Poisson distribution with rate $\lambda$, the mean and variance values are given by
$$
E(X) = \lambda, \, Var(X) = \lambda.
$$
In our ER example, by the Poisson approximation, we can compute the standard deviation of the number of patients arriving:
$$
\sigma = \sqrt{20} = 4.47.
$$

### Probability Density Function

Consider a random experiment where a continuous random variable $X$ is observed such as the location of the spinner in Section \ref{sec:Uniform}.  Define the support of $X$ to be the set of possible values  for $X$.  For example, the support of $X$ for the spinner example is the interval (0, 100).  To describe probabilities about $X$,  a _probability density function_\index{probability density!function} (pdf) denoted by $f(x)$ is defined.  Any function $f$ will not work -- we require that $f$ satisfy two properties:

**Property 1**.  The probability density function\index{density} $f$ must be **nonnegative** which means that 
\begin{equation}
 f(x) \ge 0, { for\,  all\,}  x.
\end{equation}
 
**Property 2**.  The total area under the probability density density\index{density!curve} $f$ must be equal to 1. Mathematically,
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) dx = 1.
\end{equation}

### Waiting for a Bus

To illustrate a probability density function, suppose that a professor has a class that meets three times a week.  To get to class, the professor walks to a bus stop and wait for a bus to go to school.  From past experience, the professor knows that she can wait any time between 0 and 10 minutes for the bus, and she knows that each waiting time between 0 and 10 minutes is equally likely\index{equally likely}.
For a given week, what's the chance that her longest wait will be under 7 minutes?  

Let $W$ denote her longest waiting time for the week. In thinking about plausible values of $W$, it is clear that $W$ ranges between 0 and 10 minutes and large values of $W$ are more likely. A probability density function for $W$ that reflects these beliefs is given by
$$
f(w) = \frac{3w^2}{1000}, 0 < w < 10.
$$
This pdf for this longest waiting time is shown in @fig-waiting1.

```{r}
#| echo: false
#| warning: false
#| label: fig-waiting1
#| fig-cap: "Density curve for the longest waiting time $W$."
library(ggplot2)
library(dplyr)
crcblue <- "#2905a1"
df <- data.frame(w = seq(0, 10, length = 200)) |> 
      mutate(Density = 3 * w ^ 2 / 1000 )
ggplot(df, aes(w, Density)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1,
            color = crcblue) +
  xlab("Waiting Time")
```

Before we go any further, we should check if this is indeed a legitimate probability density function:

1. Note from the graph that the density does not take on negative values, so the first property is satisfied.
2. Second, for it to be a probability density function, the entire area under the curve must be equal to 1.  One can check this by finding the integral of the density between 0 and 10 (the region where the density if positive):
$$
\int_0^{10} \frac{3w^2}{1000} dw = \frac{w^3}{1000}\Big|^{10}_0 = \frac{10^3}{1000} - \frac{0^3}{1000} = 1.
$$
 
The entire area under the curve is indeed equal to 1, so $f$ is a legitimate probability density function.
Now that $f$ is known to be a probability density function, we can use it to find probabilities.  To find the probability that this longest waiting time is less than 7 minutes, $P(W < 7)$, we wishe to compute the area under the density curve between 0 and 7, as shown in @fig-waiting2.

```{r}
#| echo: false
#| warning: false
#| label: fig-waiting2
#| fig-cap: "Density curve for the longest waiting time $W$, and $P(W < 7)$."

crcblue <- "#2905a1"
df <- data.frame(w = seq(0, 10, length = 200)) |> 
      mutate(Density = 3 * w ^ 2 / 1000 )
w <- seq(0, 7, length = 200)  
w1 <- c(w, 7, 0)
fw <- 3 * w ^ 2 / 1000
fw1 <- c(fw, 0, 0)
ggplot(df, aes(w, Density)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1,
            color = "black") +
  xlab("Waiting Time") +
  geom_polygon(data = data.frame(w1, fw1), aes(w1, fw1), 
        fill = crcblue)
```

This is equivalent to the integral
$$
P(0 < W < 7) = \int_0^{7} \frac{3w^2}{1000} dw 
$$
and, by evaluating this, we obtain the probability
 $$
P(0 < W < 7) = \int_0^{7} \frac{3w^2}{1000} dw = \frac{w^3}{1000}\Big|^{7}_0 = \frac{7^3}{1000} - \frac{0^3}{1000} = 0.343.
$$

Suppose we are interested in the probability that the longest waiting time is between 6 and 8 minutes.    This is represented by the shaded area in @fig-waiting3.

```{r}

#| warning: false
#| label: fig-waiting3
```

To compute this area, we find the integral of the density between 6 and 8:
 $$
P(6 < W < 8) = \int_6^{8} \frac{3w^2}{1000} dw = \frac{w^3}{1000}\Big|^{8}_6 = \frac{8^3}{1000} - \frac{6^3}{1000} = 0.296.
$$

Since a continuous random variable assigns zero probability to single values, this probability is not changed if we include the endpoints of the interval:

$$
P(6 \le W \le 8) = P(6 < W < 8) = 0.296.
$$

### The Cumulative Distribution Function\index{cumulative!distribution function}

Consider an arbitrary random variable $X$ with pdf $f(x)$.
Choose an arbitrary point $x$ -- the _cumulative distribution function_ at $x$, or cdf for short, is the probability that $X$ is less than or equal to $x$:
\begin{equation}
F(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(x) dx.
\end{equation}

Consider the random variable $W$, the longest waiting time in the bus waiting example.  Suppose we choose a value of $x$ in the interval (0, 10).  Then $F(x)$ would be the area under the density curve between 0 and $x$ shown in @fig-waiting4.

```{r}
#| echo: false
#| warning: false
#| label: fig-waiting4
#| fig-cap: "Illustration of the cumulative density function of $W$ for the bus waiting example."
library(ggplot2)
library(dplyr)
crcblue <- "#2905a1"
df <- data.frame(w = seq(0, 10, length = 200)) |> 
      mutate(Density = 3 * w ^ 2 / 1000 )
w <- seq(0, 6, length = 200)  
w1 <- c(w, 6, 0)
fw <- 3 * w ^ 2 / 1000
fw1 <- c(fw, 0, 0)
ggplot(df, aes(w, Density)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1,
            color = crcblue) +
  xlab("Waiting Time") +
  geom_polygon(data = data.frame(w1, fw1), aes(w1, fw1), 
        fill = crcblue) +
  annotate(geom = "text", x = 6, y = -.01, label = "x",
           size = 5) +
  annotate(geom = "text", x = 2.5, y = .17, 
           label = "F(x) = P(W <= x)", 
           size = 5) +
  geom_segment(data = data.frame(x1 = 2.5, x2 = 4,
                                 y1 = .15, y2 = 0.05),
               aes(x = x1, y = y1, xend = x2, yend = y2))
```


Writing this area as an integral, we compute $F(x)$ as
 $$
F(x) = P(W \le x) = \int_0^x \frac{3w^2}{1000} dw = \frac{w^3}{1000}\Big|^{x}_0 = \frac{x^3}{1000}.
$$
This formula is valid for any value of  $x$ in the interval (0, 10).

In fact, $F(x)$ is defined for all values of $x$ on the real line.

- If $x$ is a value smaller or equal to 0, then we see from the figure that the probability that $W$ is smaller than $x$ is equal to 0.  So $F(x) = 0$ for $x \le 0$.

-   On the other hand, if $x$ is greater or equal to 10, then the probability that $W$ is smaller than $x$ is 1.  So $F(x) = 1$ for $x \ge 10$.

Putting it all together, we see that the cdf $F$ is given by
$$
F(x) = 
 \begin{cases}
                                   0, & x \le 0  \\
                                   x^3 / 1000, & 0 < x < 10 \\
  1, & x \ge 10.
  \end{cases}
$$
 
 
Once we have computed the cdf $F$, probabilities are found by evaluating $F$ at different points.  For example, to find the probability that the maximum waiting time $W$ is less than equal to 6 minutes, we just compute $F(6) = P(W \le 6) = 6^3 / 1000 = 0.216$. @fig-waiting5 displays the cdf function and illustrates the computation.

$$
 Likewise, if we are interested in the chance that the waiting time $W$ falls between 2 and 4,  represent the probability as the difference of two "less-than" probabilities, and then subtract the two values of F.
$$
  P(2 < W < 4) = P(W \le 4) - P(W \le 2)  = F(4) - F(2) = \frac{4^3}{1000} - \frac{2^3}{1000} = 0.056.
$$
  
### Mean, Standard Deviation and Percentile of a Continuous Random Variable


One is interested in summarizing a continuous random variable.  Natural summaries are given by the mean $\mu$ and the standard deviation $\sigma$, where these quantities are defined in a similar manner as for a discrete random variable, with the exception that summations are replaced by integrals.

The mean $\mu$, or equivalently the expected value of $X$, is given by
\begin{equation}
\mu = E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x f(x) dx.
\end{equation}
Just as in the discrete random variable case, there is an attractive interpretation of $\mu$.  If we are able to observe a large number of values of $X$, then $\mu$ will be approximately equal to the sample mean\index{sample!mean} $\bar X$  of these random values of $X$.

To define the spread of the values of $X$, we first compute the average squared deviation about the mean, the variance\index{variance}, 
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2 = Var(X) = E(X - \mu)^2 = \int_{-\infty}^\infty (x - \mu)^2 f(x) dx.
\end{equation}
The standard deviation of $X$, $\sigma$, is defined to be the square root of the variance.

Let's illustrate the computation of the mean and standard deviation for the bus waiting time problem.  Using the definition of $f$, we get that the mean is equal to 
 $$
 \mu = \int_0^{10} x \left(\frac{3x^2}{1000}\right) dx.
 $$
Performing the integration, we obtain
$$
 \mu = \int_0^{10} x \left(\frac{3x^2}{1000}\right) dx = \frac{3 x^4}{4000} \Big|^{10}_0 = \frac{3 (10)^4}{1000} = 7.5.
$$
On, the average, we expect the longest wait in a week to be 7.5 minutes.

The computation of the variance is a bit more tedious, but straightforward.  
  $$
 \sigma^2 = \int_0^{10} (x -\mu)^2  \left(\frac{3x^2}{1000}\right) dx = 3.75.
 $$
So the standard deviation of $X$ is  $\sigma = \sqrt{3.75} = 1.94$.



Another useful summary of a continuous random variable is a percentile.  The 70th percentile, for example, is the value of $X$, call it $x$, such that 70\% of the probability is to the left, shown in @fig-waiting6.  That is, the 70th percentile, call it $x_{70}$, satisfies the equation
$$
P(X \le x_{70}) = 0.70.
$$

 A Normal density curve\index{Normal!density} has the general form
\begin{equation}
 f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi} \sigma} \exp\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right\}, -\infty < x < \infty.
 \end{equation}
This density curve is described by two parameters -- the mean $\mu$ and the standard deviation $\sigma$.  The mean $\mu$ is the center of the curve.  The mean of the Normal curve fit to the marathon time data   is $\mu$ = 229.  The number $\sigma$, the standard deviation, describes the spread of the curve.  Here the Normal curve standard deviation is $\sigma$ = 47.  If we know the mean and standard deviation of the Normal curve, we can make reasonable predictions where the majority of times of the women runners will fall.


Suppose that the Normal density with $\mu$ = 229 minutes and $\sigma$= 47 minutes represents the distribution of  racing times.  Say we are interested in the probability that a runner completes the race less than 4 hours or 240 minutes.  One computes this probability by finding an area under the Normal curve.  Specifically, as indicated in @fig-normal3, this probability is the area under the curve for all times less than 240 minutes.
 
```{r}
#| echo: false
#| warning: false
#| label: fig-normal3
#| fig-cap: "Normal(229, 47) density with illustration of the area under the curve less than 240 (minutes)."
library(ProbBayes)
crcblue <- "#2905a1"
normal_area(-Inf, 240, c(229, 47),
            Color = crcblue) +
  xlab("Completion Time")
```


**Normal Probability Calculations**
  
  One expresses this area as the integral
$$
  P(X \le 240) = \int_{-\infty}^{240} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi} \sigma} \exp\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right\} dx,
$$
    but unfortunately we cannot integrate this function analytically (like was done for a Uniform density) to find the probability.  Instead we  find this area by use of the R  function \texttt{pnorm()}\index{pnorm()} that gives values of the Normal cdf for any values of the mean and standard deviation. This function is used for the following three examples, illustrating the computation of three types of areas.
  
  Returning to our example, recall the distribution of marathon times is approximately Normally distributed with mean $\mu$ = 229 and standard deviation $\sigma$ = 47.  
  
  1. **Finding a "less than" area.**
    Suppose we are interested in the probability that a woman marathon runner completes the race in under 240 minutes.  
  That is, we wish to find $P(X < 240)$ which is the area under the Normal curve to the left of 240.  The function value \texttt{pnorm(x, m, s)} gives the value of the cdf of a Normal random variable\index{Normal} with mean $\mu = m$ and $\sigma = s$ evaluated at the value $x$.  For our example, the mean and standard deviation are given by 274 and 43, respectively, so the desired probability is given by

```{r}
pnorm(240, 229, 47)
```
  
  2. **Finding a "between two values" area.**  Suppose we are interested in computing the probability that a marathon runner completes a race between two values, such as $P(230 < X < 280)$, shown in @fig-normal4.

```{r}
#| echo: false
#| warning: false
#| label: fig-normal4
#| fig-cap: "Display of Normal(229, 47) density with illustration of the area under the curve greater than 300 (minutes)."
library(ProbBayes)
crcblue <- "#2905a1"
normal_area(230, 280, c(229, 47),
           Color = crcblue) +
  xlab("Completion Time")
```


One  writes this probability as the difference of two "less than" probabilities:
    \begin{align*}
  P(230 < X < 280)  &= P(X < 280) - P(X < 230) \\
  &= F(280) - F(230), 
  \end{align*}
  where $F(x)$ is the cdf of a Normal(274, 43) random variable evaluated at $x$.  Therefore, by use of the \texttt{pnorm()} function, this probability is equal to

```{r}
pnorm(280, 229, 47) - pnorm(230, 229, 47)
```
  
  3. **Finding a "greater than" area.** Last, sometimes we will be interested in the probability that $X$ is greater than some value, such as $P(X > 300)$, the probability a  runner takes more than 300 minutes to complete the race, shown in @fig-normal5.
  

```{r}
#| echo: false
#| warning: false
#| label: fig-normal5
#| fig-cap: "Normal(229, 47) density with illustration of the area under the curve greater than 300 (minutes)."

            Color = crcblue) +
  xlab("Completion Time")
```

  
  This probability is found by the complement property\index{complement!property} of probability, that 
  \begin{align*}
  P(X > 300)  &= 1 - P(X \le 300) \\
  &= 1 - F(300).
  \end{align*}
  Therefore, we use the \texttt{pnorm()} function to compute the probability that $X$ is smaller than 300, and then subtract the answer from 1.

```{r}
1 - pnorm(300, 229, 47) 
```

### Binomial Probabilities and the Normal Curve\index{Binomial!probability}\index{Normal!curve}\index{Normal approximation to the Binomial}

The Normal curve is useful for modeling batches of data, especially when we are collecting measurements of some process.  But actually there is a more important justification for the Normal curve.  We will explore several important results about the pattern of Binomial probabilities and sample means\index{sample!mean} and we will find these results useful in our introduction to statistical inference in later chapters.

First, consider different shapes of Binomial distributions.  Suppose that half of the student body lives on-campus and a sample survey of $n$ students is conduct to learn if the study body is interested in using a new recreational sports complex that is proposed.  Let $X$ denote the number of on-campus students in the sample. Assuming a random sample is chosen, it is known that $X$ will be distributed Binomial with parameters $n$ and $p=0.5$.  What is the shape of the Binomial probabilities?  @fig-normal8 displays the Binomial probabilities for sample sizes $n$ = 10, 20, 50, and 100.

Suppose that only 10% of all students would use the new facility and let $X$ denote the number of students in your sample who say they would use the facility.   \begin{equation}
  Z = \frac{X - n p}{\sqrt{n p (1 - p)}}
  \end{equation}
  approaches a standard Normal random variable, that is a Normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.  This is a very useful result.   \begin{equation}
  \mu = n p, \, \, \, \sigma = \sqrt{n p (1 - p)}.
  \end{equation}

## Joint Probability Mass Function{#sec-jointpmf}

Up to this point, we have been considers experiments where only one random variable is observed.  But certainly, it is common to collect several random variables in experiments.  For example, in a running race, we may collect the identity and time for the winning runner.  We want to extend our description of probabilities to situations when two variables are observed.

To begin the discussion of two random variables, we start with an example of balls in a box. Consider a box of ten balls -- four are white, three are red, and three are black. Five balls are selected out of the box without replacement\index{without replacement} and the number of white and red balls in the sample are counted. What is the probability two white and two red balls are observed in the sample?

1.  First, we think about the total number of ways of selecting five balls with replacement from a box of ten balls. One assumes the balls are distinct and the order that of the ball selection does not matter.  Then the total number of outcomes is 
$$
     N = {10 \choose 5} = 252.
     $$

2.  Next, we think about the number of ways of selecting two white and two red balls. One does this in steps -- first select the white balls, then select the red balls, and then select the one remaining black ball. Note that five balls are selected, so exactly one of the balls must be black. Since the box has four white balls, the number of ways of choose two white is ${4 \choose 2} = 6$. Of the three red balls, we want to choose two -- the number of ways of doing that is ${3 \choose 2} = 3$. Last, the number of ways of choosing the remaining one black ball is ${3 \choose 1} = 3.$ So the total number of ways of choosing two white, two red, and one black ball is the product 
$$
     {4 \choose 2} \times {3 \choose 2} \times {3 \choose 1} = 6 \times 3 \times 3 = 54.
     $$

3.  Each one of the ${10 \choose 5} = 252$ possible outcomes of five balls is equally likely\index{equally likely} to be chosen. Of these outcomes, 54 resulted in two white and two red balls, so the probability of choosing two white and two red balls is $$
     P({2 \, white \, and \, 2 \, red}) = \frac{54}{252}.
     $$

Here the probability of choosing a specific number of white and red balls has been found. To do this calculation for other outcomes, it is convenient to define two random variables

$X$ = number of red balls selected, $Y$ = number of white balls selected.

Based on what was found, $$
 P(X = 2, Y = 2) = \frac{54}{252}.
 $$

**Joint Probability Mass Function**\index{joint!probability!mass function}

Suppose this calculation is done for every possible pair of values of $X$ and $Y$. The table of probabilities is given in @tbl-jointpmf.

|                 |       |        |        |        |       |

This table is called the _joint probability mass function_ (pmf) $f(x, y)$ of ($X, Y$).\
As for any probability distribution, we require that each of the probability values are nonnegative and the sum of the probabilities over all values of $X$ and $Y$ is one. That is, the function $f(x, y)$ satisfies two properties:

1.  $f(x, y) \ge 0$, for all $x$, $y$
2.  $\Sigma_{x, y} f(x, y) = 1$

It is clear from @tbl-jointpmf that all of the probabilities are nonnegative and the reader can confirm that the sum of the probabilities is equal to one.

Using @tbl-jointpmf, we see that some particular pairs $(x, y)$ are not possible as $f(x, y) = 0$. For example, $f(0, 1) = 0$ which means that it is not possible to observe 0 red balls and 1 white ball in the sample. Note that five balls were sampled, and if we only observed one red or white ball, that means that we must have sampled $5 - 1 = 4$ black balls which is not possible.

We find probabilities of any event involving $X$ and $Y$ by summing probabilities from @tbl-jointpmf.

-   What is$P(X = Y)$, the probability that we sample the same number of red and white balls? By the table, we see that this is possible only when $X = 1, Y = 1$ or $X = 2, Y = 2$. So the probability $$
    P(X = Y) = f(1, 1) + f(2, 2) = \frac{12}{252} + \frac{54}{252} = \frac{66}{252}.
    $$
-   What is $P(X > Y)$, the probability we sample more red balls than white balls? From the table, we identify the outcomes where $X > Y$, and then sum the corresponding probabilities. \begin{align*}
    P(X > Y) & = f(1, 0) + f(2, 0) + f(2, 1) + f(3, 0) + f(3, 1) + f(3, 2) \\
    & = \frac{12}{252} + \frac{3}{252} + \frac{36}{252} + \frac{3}{252} + \frac{12}{252} + \frac{6}{252} \\

    \end{align*}

## Joint Probability Density Functions{#sec-jointpdf}

One can also describe probabilities when the two variables $X$ and $Y$ are continuous. As a simple example, suppose that we randomly choose two points $X$ and $Y$ on the interval (0, 2) where $X < Y$. One defines the _joint probability density function_ or joint pdf of $X$ and $Y$ to be the function $$
 f(x, y) =
 \begin{cases}
\frac{1}{2},  \, \, 0  < x < y <2;\\
0, \, \,{elsewhere}.
\end{cases}
$$

```{r fig-jointpdfregion, echo = FALSE, out.width = 300, fig.cap = "Region where the joint pdf $f(x, y)$ is positive in the choose two points example."}
require(ggplot2)
crcblue <- "#2905a1"
ggplot() +
  geom_polygon(data = data.frame(x = c(0, 0, 2, 0),
                              y = c(0, 2, 2, 0)),
            mapping = aes(x, y),
            fill = crcblue,
            alpha = 0.2) +
  theme(text=element_text(size=18)) +
  coord_equal(ratio = 1)
```

This joint pdf is viewed as a plane of constant height over the set of points $(x, y)$ where $0  < x < y <2$. This region of points in the plane is shown in @fig-jointpdfregion.

In the one variable situation in Section 2.3, a function $f$ is a legitimate density function or pdf if it is nonnegative over the real line and the total area under the curve is equal to one. Similarly for two variables, any function $f(x, y)$ is considered a pdf if it satisfies two properties: \newpage

1.  The density is nonnegative over the whole plane: \begin{equation}
    f(x, y) \ge 0, \, \, {for} \, {all} \, \, x, y.
    \end{equation}
2.  The total volume under the density is equal to one: \begin{equation}
    \int \int f(x, y) dx dy = 1.
    \end{equation}

The density in our example is indeed a legitimate joint pdf. It is pretty obvious that the density that was defined is nonnegative, but it is less clear that the integral of the density is equal to one. Since the density is a plane of constant height, we compute this double integral geometrically. Using the familiar \`\`one half base times height" argument, the area of the triangle in the plane is $(1/2) (2) (2) = 2$ and since the pdf has constant height of $1/2$, the volume under the surface is equal to $2 (1/2)  =1$.  One can also compute the double integral as follows to confirm that the total volume is equal to one.
\begin{align*}
 \int_{0}^2 \int^y_{3-y} f(x, y) dx dy
 & = \int_{0}^2 \int^y_{3-y} \frac{1}{2} dx dy \\
 & = \int_{0}^2 \frac{2 y - 3}{2}dy \\
 & = \frac{y^2 - 3 y}{ 2} \Big|_{0}^2 \\
 & = \frac{2^3 - 3 (2)}{2} = 1.
 \end{align*}

Probabilities about $X$ and $Y$ are found by finding volumes under the pdf surface. For example, suppose we wish to find the probability that the sum of locations $X + Y > 3$, that is $P(X + Y > 3)$. The region in the $(x, y)$ plane of interest is first identified, and then we find the volume under the joint pdf over this region.

```{r fig-shadedregion, echo = FALSE, out.width = 300, fig.cap = "Shaded region where x + y > 3 in the choose two points example."}

  geom_polygon(data = data.frame(x = c(0, 0, 2, 0),
                                 y = c(0, 2, 2, 0)),
               mapping = aes(x, y),
               fill = crcblue,
               alpha = 0.2) +
  geom_polygon(data = data.frame(x = c(1, 1.5, 2, 1),
                                 y = c(2, 1.5, 2, 2)),
               mapping = aes(x, y),
               fill = crcblue) +
  theme(text=element_text(size=18)) +
  coord_equal(ratio = 1)
```

In @fig-shadedregion, the region where $x + y > 3$ has been shaded. The probability $P(X + Y > 3)$ is the volume under the joint pdf over this region. Applying a geometric argument, we note that the area of the shaded region is $1/4$, and so the probability of interest is $(1/4)(1/2) = 1/8$. One also finds this probability by integrating the joint pdf over the region as follows: \begin{align*}
 P(X + Y < 3) & = \int_{1.5}^2 \int^y_{3-y} f(x, y) dx dy \\
 & = \int_{1.5}^2 \int^y_{3-y} \frac{1}{2} dx dy \\
 & = \int_{1.5}^2 \frac{2 y - 3}{2}dy \\
 & = \frac{y^2 - 3 y}{ 2} \Big|_{1.5}^2 \\
 & = \frac{1}{8}.
 \end{align*}

## Marginal Probability Functions{#sec-marginal}

### Discrete Random Variables

Once a joint probability mass function for $(X, Y)$ has been constructed, we find probabilities for one of the two variables. In our balls-in-box example in Section 2.4, suppose we want to find the probability that exactly three red balls are chosen, that is $P(X = 3)$. This probability is found by summing values of the joint pmf $f(x, y)$ where $x = 3$ and $y$ can be any possible value of the random variable $Y$, that is, \begin{align*}
 P(X = 3) & = \sum_y f(3, y) \\
 & = f(3, 0) + f(3, 1) + f(3, 2) \\
 & = \frac{3}{252} + \frac{12}{252} + \frac{6}{252} \\
 & = \frac{21}{252}.
 \end{align*}

This operation is done for each of the possible values of $X$ -- the *marginal* probability mass function\index{marginal!probability!mass function} of $X$, $f_X()$ is defined as follows: \begin{equation}
 f_X(x) = \sum_y f(x, y).
 \end{equation} One finds this marginal pmf of $X$ from @tbl-jointpmf by summing the joint probabilities for each row of the table. The marginal pmf of $X$ is displayed in @tbl-marginalX.

| $x$ | $f_X(x)$ |
|:---:|:--------:|
|  0  |  21/252  |
|  1  | 105/252  |
|  2  | 105/252  |
|  3  |  21/252  |

: Marginal pmf for $X$ in balls in box example. {#tbl-marginalX}

Note that a marginal pmf is a legitimate probability function in that the values are nonnegative and the probabilities sum to one.

We can also find the marginal pmf of $Y$, denoted by $f_Y()$, by a similar operation -- for a fixed value of $Y = y$ we sum over all of the possible values of $X$. \begin{equation}
 f_Y(y) = \sum_x f(x, y).
\end{equation}

For example, if we want to find $f_Y(2) = P(Y = 2)$ in our example, we sum the joint probabilities in @tbl-jointpmf over the rows in the column where $Y = 2$. One obtains the probability: \begin{align*}
 f_Y(2) & = \sum_x f(x, 2) \\
 & = f(0, 2) + f(1, 2) + f(2, 2) + f(3, 2)\\
 & = \frac{6}{252} + \frac{54}{252} + \frac{54}{252} + \frac{6}{252}\\
 & = \frac{120}{252}.
 \end{align*} By repeating this exercise for each value of $Y$, we obtain the marginal pmf of $Y$ displayed in @tbl-marginalY.

### Continuous Random Variables

Given a joint pdf $f(x, y)$ that describes probabilities of two continuous variables $X$ and $Y$, we summarize probabilities about each variable individually by the computation of marginal pdfs. The _marginal pdf_ of $X$, $f_X(x)$, is obtained by integrating out $y$ from the joint pdf. \begin{equation}
f_X(x) = \int f(x, y) dy.
\end{equation} In a similar fashion, we define the marginal pdf of $Y$ by integrating out $x$ from the joint pdf. \begin{equation}
f_Y(x) = \int f(x, y) dx.
\end{equation}

Let's illustrate the computation of marginal pdfs for the example of Section 2.5. One has to be careful about the limits of the integration due to the dependence between $x$ and $y$ in the support\index{support} of the joint density. Looking back at @fig-jointpdfregion, we see that if the value of $x$ is fixed, then the limits for $y$ go from $x$ to 2. So the marginal density of $X$ is given by \begin{align*}
 f_X(x) & = \int f(x, y) dy \\
 & = \int_x^2  \frac{1}{2}  dy \\
 & = \frac{2 - x}{2}, \, \, 0 < x < 2.
 \end{align*} By a similar calculation, we can verify that the marginal density of $Y$ is equal to $$
 f_Y(y) = \frac{y}{2}, \, \, 0 < y < 2.
 $$

## Conditional Probability Functions{#sec-conditional}

### Discrete Random Variables

In the sampling balls from a box example from Section 2.4 and Section 2.6.1 , suppose we are told that exactly two red balls are sampled, that is $X$ = 2 -- how does that information change the probabilities about the number of white balls $Y$?

In this example, we are interested in finding $P(Y = y \mid X = 2)$. Using the definition of conditional probability, we have \begin{align*}
 P(Y = y \mid X = 2) & = \frac{P(Y = y, X = 2)}{P(X = 2)}. \\
 & = \frac{f(2, y)}{f_X(2)}
 \end{align*} For example, the probability of observing two white balls given that we have two red balls is equal to \begin{align*}
 P(Y = 2 \mid X = 2) & = \frac{P(Y = 2, X = 2)}{P(X = 2)} \\
 & = \frac{f(2, 2)}{f_X(2)} \\
 & = \frac{54 / 252}{105/252} = \frac{54}{105}. \\
 \end{align*}

Suppose this calculation is repeated for all possible values of $Y$ -- we obtain the values displayed in @tbl-conditionalY.

| $y$ | $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid X = 2)$ |
|:---:|:----------------------------:|
|  0  |            3/105             |
|  1  |            36/105            |
|  2  |            54/105            |
|  3  |            12/105            |

: Conditional pmf for $Y$ given $X = 2$ in balls in box example. {#tbl-conditionalY}

These probabilities represent the _conditional probability mass function_ for $Y$ conditional on $X = 2$. This conditional pmf is just like any other probability distribution in that the values are nonnegative and they sum to one. To illustrate using this distribution, suppose we are told that two red balls are selected (that is, $X = 2$) and we want to find the probability that more than one white ball is chosen. This probability is given by \begin{align*}
 P(Y > 1 \mid X = 2) & = \Sigma_{y >1} \, \, f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid X = 2) \\
 & = f_{Y \mid X}(2 \mid X = 2) + f_{Y \mid X}(3 \mid X = 2) \\
 & = \frac{54}{105} + \frac{12}{105} = \frac{66}{105}.
 \end{align*}

In general, the conditional pmf of $Y$ conditional on $X = x$, denoted by $f_{Y\mid X}(y \mid x)$, is defined to be \begin{equation}
 f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)  = \frac{f(x, y)}{f_X(x)}, \, \, { if} \, \, f_X(x) > 0.
 \end{equation}

### Continuous Random Variables

One can also define conditional probability densities in the continuous random variable. Returning to our choose two points example in Section 2.5 and 2.6.2, suppose we are told that the first random location is equal to $X = 1.5$. What have we learned about the value of the second random variable $Y$?

To answer this question, we define the notion of a conditional pdf. The conditional pdf of the random variable $Y$ given the value $X = x$ is defined as the quotient \begin{equation}
f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid X = x) = \frac{f(x, y)}{f_X(x)}, \, \, { if} \, \, f_X(x) > 0.
\end{equation} In our example we are given that $X = 1.5$. Looking at @fig-jointpdfregion, we see that when $X = 1.5$, the only possible values of $Y$ are between 1.5 and 2. By substituting the values of $f(x, y)$ and $f_X(x)$, we obtain \begin{align*}
 f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid X = 1.5) & = \frac{f(1.5, y)}{f_X(1.5)} \\
 & =  \frac{1/2}{(2 - 1.5) / 2} \\
 & =  2, \, \, 1.5 < y < 2. \\
 \end{align*} In other words, the conditional density for $Y$ when $X = 1.5$ is uniform from 1.5 to 2.

A conditional pdf is a legitimate density function, so the integral of the conditional pdf of $Y$ given a value of $X$ over all values $y$ is equal to one. We can use this density to compute conditional probabilities. For example, if $X$ = 1.5, what is the probability that $Y$ is greater than 1.7? This probability is the conditional probability $P(Y > 1.7 \mid X = 1.5)$ that is equal to an integral over the conditional density $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid 1.5)$: \begin{align*}
 P(Y > 1.7 \mid X = 1.5) & = \int_{1.7}^2 f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid 1.5) dy \\
 & =  \int_{1.7}^2 2 dy \\
 & =  0.6. \\
 \end{align*}

**Turn the Random Variables Around**

Above, we looked at the pdf of $Y$ conditional on a value of $X$. One can also consider a pdf of $X$ conditional on a value of $Y$. Returning to our example, suppose that we learn that $Y$, the larger random variable on the interval is equal to 0.8. In this case, what would we expect for the random variable $X$?

This question can be answered in two steps -- we first find the conditional pdf of $X$ conditional on $Y = 0.8$. Then once this conditional pdf is found, we find the mean of this distribution.

The conditional pdf of $X$ given the value $Y = y$ is defined as the quotient \begin{equation}
f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid Y = y) = \frac{f(x, y)}{f_Y(y)}, \, \, { if} \, \, f_Y(y) > 0.
\end{equation} Looking back at @fig-jointpdfregion, we see that if $Y = 0.8$, the possible values of $X$ are from 0 to 0.8. Over these values the conditional pdf of $X$ is given by \begin{align*}
 f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid Y = 0.8) & = \frac{f(x, 0.8)}{f_Y(0.8)} \\
 & =  \frac{1/2}{0.8 / 2} \\
 & =  1.25, \, \, 0 < x < 0.8. \\
 \end{align*} So if we know that $Y = 0.8$, then the conditional pdf for $X$ is Uniform on (0, 0.8).

To find the expected value of $X$ knowing that $Y = 0.8$, we find the mean of this distribution. \begin{align*}
 E(X \mid Y = 0.8) & = \int_0^{0.8} x f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid 0.8) dx \\
 & = \int_0^{0.8} x \,1.25 \, dx \\ \\
 & =  (0.8)^2 / 2 \times 1.25 = 0.4. \\
 \end{align*}
